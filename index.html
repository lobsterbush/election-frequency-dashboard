How often nations vote, and what it does to them

A country-year record of national elections since 1945, and a first look at whether elections change political division, indifference, mobilization, and the quality of democracy. The story comes in three parts: how often the world votes, what voting more often does, and what holding an election does. Explore each below.

Part 3

What does holding an election do?

Now the treatment is a single election year. What changes when a country goes to the polls? Not division, it turns out, but mobilization, democratic quality, and the mix depends on the regime.

What an election does to division

A country-year model with country and year fixed effects, tracing political division in the years around a national election. If elections drove division, the jump would land at the election year. Instead division drifts up before the vote too, so this looks like a build-up, not an effect of the election itself. Hover for the numbers.

Descriptive within-country associations, not causal estimates. Election timing is endogenous (snap elections come in crises), and the pre-election rise signals anticipation.

Beyond division: what else an election moves

The same country-year model, run for five annual outcomes, each standardised. Positive means more of the outcome in an election year. Elections mobilize, and sometimes inflame, but they do not clearly divide. Hover for the robustness across specifications.

Descriptive within-country associations, not causal estimates. Each row is one workhorse model; the hover shows how often the effect is positive and significant across the specifications for that outcome.

Does an election improve democracy?

V-Dem's Electoral Democracy Index and its five sub-indices, in an election year versus a normal one. Because the outcome is democracy, these models control for income and education but not for democracy itself. Elections coincide with gains almost everywhere, most of all in filling elected offices.

Descriptive within-country associations, not causal: democracies hold more elections, so read these as gains that coincide with elections, not gains they cause.

Where do elections matter most?

The same election effect, re-estimated within each Regimes-of-the-World category, from closed autocracy to liberal democracy. Each small panel is one outcome; each dot an effect for one regime type, with its 95% interval. The pattern the project cares about is the hump in mass mobilization: elections mobilize most in the contested middle, electoral autocracies and electoral democracies, and least at the two extremes.

Descriptive within-country associations, not causal. Reading left to right in each panel moves from closed autocracy to liberal democracy; a solid dot is significant at 5%, a faded one is not.

What's behind the numbers

Everything here is real, drawn straight from the analysis outputs. Sources are attributed; nothing is invented.

National election
Elections choosing national legislative or executive representatives. Same-day contests count once (one polling occasion per day). Referendums excluded.
Frequency
Rolling count of polling occasions over the past ten years.
Division
V-Dem political polarization (v2cacamps), higher means more divided.
Indifference
Voter turnout, share of registered voters. Lower turnout is the proxy.
Sources
NELDA 6.0 (1945–2020, authoritative); International IDEA for the post-2020 extension, with IFES ElectionGuide filling the country-years IDEA lacks (held elections only, to 2026); V-Dem v14 (outcomes and covariates); World Bank (robustness covariates).
Cross-check
The three election sources agree closely where they overlap: NELDA vs IDEA and NELDA vs ElectionGuide before 2020 (both 98% within one election), and IDEA vs ElectionGuide after 2020 (93% exact, 99% within one), so the sources reinforce rather than pull against the counts.
Inference
Country and year fixed effects; standard errors clustered by country; missing values multiply imputed with Rubin pooling.
Caveat
Descriptive associations, not causal estimates. Frequency is endogenous to regime type.
